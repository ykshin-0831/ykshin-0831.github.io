AI Guard: Validating a Portfolio Risk Engine

VaR backtesting, ablation, pre-specified model selection, code verification and a study of breach clustering

Artificial Intelligence & Business Model Laboratory (Prof. Kyoung Jun Lee), team lead of six, September 2025 to present

My role
I led the six-member team building the prototype, designed and ran its statistical validation, re-implemented the estimators and backtest, and ran the clustering study.
Product
Recomputes a portfolio’s one-day VaR and CVaR in Korean won as users add or remove holdings, for individual investors and the institutions that serve them
Data
Daily adjusted closes: a KOSPI 200 ETF (KODEX 200, 2,562 forecast days) and 33 Korean stocks forming 300 random portfolios of 3, 6 and 13 stocks (Dirichlet weights), from 2016 so that March 2020 is included. For a test on new data, 2007–2015 prices for the 19 of these stocks that pass every data check, all still listed today, so that sample leans toward survivors
Methods
EWMA volatility, Gaussian, Student-t, historical and filtered historical simulation, CAViaR, Kupiec, Christoffersen and DQ tests with Monte Carlo calibration, simulation power analysis, model confidence set, FZ0 joint VaR–CVaR scoring, pooled LightGBM and linear quantile regression, ablation, temporal split, date-block bootstrap, a pre-specified test on new data

Key results

  • The original Gaussian VaR was breached 70 times in 2,562 days against 26 expected. Filtered historical simulation cut this to 29 and replaced it in the prototype. For users, the loss figure the app says will be exceeded on 1 day in 100 had been exceeded on 2.7 days in 100; with FHS it is 1.1.
  • Chosen on 2018–22 data, it was rejected by the Kupiec test for none of the 300 random portfolios in 2023–26. The portfolios share 33 stocks and the same trading days, so these are not 300 independent tests, and each test cannot tell 1% from 1.5% (see the power analysis). On unseen 2010–15 data it kept a 1.16% breach rate. My own re-implementation reproduces the ablation table and both periods’ rejection rates.
  • Breaches still cluster in some periods. In 2023–26 almost all of it came from two double crashes, and neither faster volatility nor market-stress features removed it without losing accuracy.

Research question

AI Guard tells users how much their portfolio could lose on a bad day: a one-day 99% VaR that should be exceeded on about 1 day in 100. Is it exceeded that rarely, which modeling choice is responsible when it is not, and once the frequency is right, why do the remaining breaches arrive in clusters?

Design

Models

Every forecast for day t+1t+1 uses data through day tt only. Volatility follows the RiskMetrics recursion

σt+12=λ σt2+(1−λ) rt2,λ=0.94\sigma_{t+1}^{2}=\lambda\,\sigma_{t}^{2}+(1-\lambda)\,r_{t}^{2},\qquad \lambda=0.94

The original design used a Gaussian quantile on 250-day volatility. Filtered historical simulation (FHS) standardizes the last 500 returns by their own volatility and rescales the empirical tail by the current one:

VaRt+1G=z0.99 σt+1,z^s=rsσs\mathrm{VaR}^{\text{G}}_{t+1}=z_{0.99}\,\sigma_{t+1},\qquad \hat z_s=\frac{r_s}{\sigma_s}
VaRt+1FHS=−σt+1 Q0.01({z^s}s=t−499t)\mathrm{VaR}^{\text{FHS}}_{t+1}=-\sigma_{t+1}\,Q_{0.01}\big(\{\hat z_s\}_{s=t-499}^{t}\big)
CVaRt+1FHS=−σt+1 E[z^∣z^≤Q0.01]\mathrm{CVaR}^{\text{FHS}}_{t+1}=-\sigma_{t+1}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\hat z\mid \hat z\le Q_{0.01}\big]

Backtests

A breach is It+1=1{Lt+1>VaRt+1}I_{t+1}=\mathbb{1}\{L_{t+1}>\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1}\}. With xx breaches in nn days, the Kupiec proportion-of-failures test compares p^=x/n\hat p=x/n with p=0.01p=0.01:

LRPOF=−2ln⁡(1−p)n−x px(1−p^)n−x p^x  ∼  χ12 under H0\mathrm{LR}_{\text{POF}}=-2\ln\frac{(1-p)^{n-x}\,p^{x}}{(1-\hat p)^{n-x}\,\hat p^{x}}\;\sim\;\chi^{2}_{1}\ \text{under } H_0

The Christoffersen test checks whether a breach makes the next one more likely, through a first-order Markov chain on ItI_t. Rejection is at the 5% level.

Ablation and pre-specified selection

Seven methods separate the two ingredients of the fix, the volatility model and the tail shape, on the ETF and on 300 random portfolios. The selection rule was fixed in advance: choose the method with the lowest Kupiec rejection rate in the selection period, then evaluate it on the later period. The split was added after a backtest over the whole period, which already included 2023–26, so the later period was not unseen. What it shows is that the rule picks FHS from 2018–22 alone; FHS uses standard settings (λ = 0.94, 500 days) that were not tuned.

Verification

Look-ahead invariance

For a cutoff t0t_0, every return after t0t_0 is replaced with synthetic values, and every forecast up to the cutoff must stay unchanged, for all seven methods:

f^t(D)=f^t(D~)∀ t≤t0\hat f_{t}(D)=\hat f_{t}(\tilde D)\qquad \forall\, t\le t_0

where D~\tilde D equals the data DD up to t0t_0 and is synthetic afterwards.

Second implementation and my re-implementation

A separately written implementation, with its own unit tests for the Gaussian formula, a hand-worked FHS example, Kupiec boundary cases and look-ahead invariance, was compared with the original date by date on 301 portfolios. I then re-implemented the estimators, tests and backtest myself in six steps (portfolio returns, EWMA, seven VaR forecasts, next-day breaches, Kupiec and Christoffersen, 300 portfolios with the selection rule) and checked them against the archived returns.

Clustering study

Three stages. The clustering of FHS breaches in 2023–26 had already been seen, and it is what prompted the study. The candidate models and the pass rule were then fixed on 2018–22 before any candidate was run on 2023–26; this was recorded internally, not publicly time-stamped.

1. Calibrated clustering tests

With about 9 expected breaches per portfolio in 2023–26, textbook χ2\chi^2 cutoffs are unreliable. Each clustering statistic TT is compared with B=999B=999 simulated sequences of independent Bernoulli(0.01) breaches of the same length:

pMC=1+#{b:  Tb∗≥T}B+1p_{\text{MC}}=\frac{1+\#\{b:\;T^{*}_{b}\ge T\}}{B+1}

The statistics are the Christoffersen independence ratio and a dynamic quantile (DQ) test that regresses centered breaches on a constant, four lagged breaches and the standardized VaR:

DQ=g⊤(X⊤X)+gα(1−α),g=X⊤(I−α1),α=0.01\mathrm{DQ}=\frac{g^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{+}g}{\alpha(1-\alpha)},\qquad g=X^{\top}(I-\alpha\mathbf{1}),\qquad \alpha=0.01

2. Two explanations

  • Volatility may react too slowly. I tested FHS with λ∈{0.94, 0.90, 0.85}\lambda\in\{0.94,\,0.90,\,0.85\}, with λ\lambda chosen on 2018–22 only, by the calibrated Christoffersen rejection share.
  • The quantile may need to be modeled directly. I tested CAViaR (symmetric absolute value), fitted for each portfolio on 2018–22 by minimizing the 99% quantile loss and then frozen:
VaRt=a+b VaRt−1+c ∣rt−1∣,a≥0,  0≤b≤0.995,  c≥0\mathrm{VaR}_{t}=a+b\,\mathrm{VaR}_{t-1}+c\,|r_{t-1}|,\qquad a\ge 0,\; 0\le b\le 0.995,\; c\ge 0
min⁡a,b,c 1∣S∣∑t∈S[(1−α) (Lt−VaRt)++α (VaRt−Lt)+],S=2018–22\min_{a,b,c}\ \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{t\in S}\Big[(1-\alpha)\,(L_t-\mathrm{VaR}_t)^{+}+\alpha\,(\mathrm{VaR}_t-L_t)^{+}\Big],\qquad S=\text{2018–22}

3. Pass rule on 2023–26

A candidate improves on FHS with λ=0.94\lambda=0.94 only if all four hold:

  1. Its share of portfolios rejected by the calibrated Christoffersen test is strictly lower.
  2. Its share rejected by the calibrated DQ test is not higher.
  3. Its share rejected by the Kupiec test is not higher.
  4. Its mean 99% quantile loss is not higher.

Power of the tests

To read rejection shares built on about 9 expected breaches, I simulated how often each test of the clustering study (Kupiec, Christoffersen and the four-lag DQ) detects specific departures at n=941n=941: independent breaches at 1.5% or 2%, and breaches that keep a 1% rate but cluster, with the probability of a breach on the day after a breach raised to p11=0.10p_{11}=0.10 or 0.200.20. To keep the clustered processes at a 1% stationary rate,

p01=α (1−p11)1−α,Pr⁡(It=1)=p011−p11+p01=αp_{01}=\frac{\alpha\,(1-p_{11})}{1-\alpha},\qquad \Pr(I_t=1)=\frac{p_{01}}{1-p_{11}+p_{01}}=\alpha

Monte Carlo thresholds come from 5,000 separate null sequences, each scenario uses 2,000 fresh sequences, and the DQ test’s VaR regressor is a fixed synthetic path.

Loss-based model comparison

Coverage tests ask whether breaches occur at the right rate and independently; scoring rules ask which forecast is closer. For FHS, Student-t (4 df) and the Gaussian model on 2023–26, each day’s loss is averaged over the 300 portfolios, and every bootstrap draw resamples the same 20-day blocks of days for all models, which keeps the shocks the portfolios share.

  • Model confidence set (Hansen, Lunde and Nason, Tmax⁡T_{\max} statistic, B=999B=999): the models that cannot be shown to be worse than the others at the 95% level, with quantile loss as the primary score.
  • Joint VaR and CVaR score: FZ0, from the Fissler–Ziegel class of scores that are consistent for the pair, for loss LL, VaR qq and CVaR ss:
FZ0(L,q,s)=1{L>q} (L−q)α s+qs+ln⁡(s)−1,s≥q>0\mathrm{FZ0}(L,q,s)=\frac{\mathbb{1}\{L>q\}\,(L-q)}{\alpha\,s}+\frac{q}{s}+\ln(s)-1,\qquad s\ge q>0

The FHS CVaR averages the five lowest of the last 500 standardized returns; the parametric models use closed forms.

Market-stress quantile models

If breaches cluster because the whole market comes under stress, telling the model about market stress should help. One model is fitted on all 300 portfolios together, which gives about 346,500 training rows instead of roughly 12 tail days per portfolio. It predicts the 1% quantile of the next day’s standardized return:

zp,t+1=rp,t+1σp,t,VaRp,t+1=−σp,t q^0.01(Xp,t)z_{p,t+1}=\frac{r_{p,t+1}}{\sigma_{p,t}},\qquad \mathrm{VaR}_{p,t+1}=-\sigma_{p,t}\,\hat q_{0.01}\big(X_{p,t}\big)
  • BASE features: ∣rp,t∣|r_{p,t}|, ∣rp,t−1∣|r_{p,t-1}|, σp,t\sigma_{p,t} and the number of holdings.
  • STRESS adds three: the EWMA volatility of the KOSPI 200 index, the cross-sectional standard deviation of the holdings’ returns on day tt, and their mean pairwise correlation over the last 20 days.
  • Models: LightGBM with the quantile loss (primary) and a linear quantile regression with an L1 penalty. LightGBM’s depth and learning rate were chosen from four candidates on 2022 data using BASE only, then both versions were refitted on 2018–22 and frozen for 2023–26.

The decision rule was written down before the run (the protocol was not publicly time-stamped). STRESS improves on BASE only if all three hold:

  1. Its share of portfolios with clustered breaches is lower.
  2. The 95% interval for its quantile-loss difference from BASE lies below zero, using a 20-day block bootstrap over shared dates.
  3. Its share of portfolios rejected by Kupiec is not higher.

A cause of clustering, tested on new data

On 2018–22 data alone, a breach on a day when the market did not fall sharply was followed by more breaches than a breach on a day of a market-wide drop. That suggested a cause: stock-specific shocks persist and market-wide ones do not. The hypothesis was tested on 2010–15, a past period no earlier analysis had evaluated (a historical test, not a prospective one), under a protocol written on 2018–22 data and committed to GitHub (commit c238bb9, 17 November 2026, 15:13 KST) before the 2007–15 prices were downloaded. The commit fixes what the protocol says, and its timestamp is git’s record, not a registry’s; the run log itself is not public, and unlike the OSF preregistrations of the crash warning paper this is not a registry entry.

Day tt is a market shock day, St=1S_t=1, when the KOSPI 200 return falls more than two volatilities: rm,t/σm,t−1<−2r_{m,t}/\sigma_{m,t-1}<-2. Each portfolio-day with a following day falls into one of three groups:

  • I: a breach without a market shock (It=1, St=0I_t=1,\ S_t=0)
  • M: a breach on a market shock day (It=1, St=1I_t=1,\ S_t=1)
  • N: neither (It=0, St=0I_t=0,\ S_t=0)
PX=Pr⁡(It+1=1∣X),R=PIPN,D=PI−PMP_X=\Pr\big(I_{t+1}=1\mid X\big),\qquad R=\frac{P_I}{P_N},\qquad D=P_I-P_M

The hypothesis is supported only if both hold, with intervals from 4,999 bootstrap draws of 20-day date blocks:

  1. The lower end of the 95% interval for RR is above 1: breaches without a market shock are followed by more breaches than usual.
  2. The lower end of the 95% interval for DD is above 0: they are followed by more breaches than breaches on market shock days.

Prices from June 2007 were checked against the earlier file on 2016–18 and against the KRX daily price limit. 19 of the 33 stocks passed every check; the rest listed later, had stale prices or showed impossible moves. 300 new portfolios were drawn with the original procedure. Also fixed on 2018–22 was a remedy: after a breach without a market shock, raise the next day’s VaR by a factor of 1.45, the value with the lowest pooled quantile loss on 2018–22. The LightGBM models were applied as frozen on 2018–22.

  • With χ2\chi^2 cutoffs the null rejection rates are 6.7% for Kupiec, 1.7% for Christoffersen and 12.2% for the four-lag DQ, against 3.2%, 4.2% and 5.8% with Monte Carlo thresholds, which supports calibrating by simulation.
  • One portfolio’s Kupiec non-rejection is weak evidence: a 1.5% breach rate is caught only 24% of the time. The support for FHS’s coverage rests more on its average breach rate across portfolios, 1.16% in 2023–26, than on the 0% rejection share.
  • Coverage and clustering need separate tests: Kupiec is nearly blind to clustering and Christoffersen to a wrong breach rate. The four-lag DQ responds to both, but it detects less than a one-lag version of the same test (33% against 42% at p11=0.10p_{11}=0.10), so more lags did not bring more power.
  • The clustering rejection shares observed for FHS, 48% for Christoffersen and 33% for DQ, are about what clustered processes with p11p_{11} between 0.10 and 0.20 produce. Shared crash days make the 300 portfolios dependent, so this gives a rough scale for the clustering, not an estimate of p11p_{11}.
  • In an analysis added afterwards, I reported intervals instead of pass or fail. Pooled over the 300 portfolios, the 2023–26 breach rate is 1.16% for FHS (95% date-block interval 0.71% to 1.67%), 1.14% for pooled LightGBM BASE and 1.21% for STRESS: all consistent with 1%, but too uncertain to tell 1% from 1.5%. At the 97.5% level breaches are 2.5 times as frequent, and Kupiec detects a 1.5-fold breach rate 61% of the time instead of about a third. There FHS breached on 2.52% of days (1.91% to 3.19%), Kupiec rejected no portfolio, and 32% still clustered.

What changes is the day after a breach: the chance of another breach falls from 8.7% to 4.5%. The stress features make VaR react faster right after a shock, but size it worse across stressed periods.

  • The hypothesis was not supported. On 2010–15 a breach without a market shock was again followed by about 2.7 times the usual breach rate, but the 95% interval reached down to 0.98, and three quarters of those repeat breaches followed just five dates. Breaches on market shock days were not clearly less persistent. Neither condition held.
  • Market crashes came in chains only in some periods. After a breach on a market shock day, the next-day breach rate was 0.5% in 2018–22, 1.8% in 2010–15 and 13.2% in 2023–26. Much of the heavy clustering of 2023–26 came from such chains, the kind of persistence the hypothesis had ruled out.
  • Broken down by date after the results were in (an exploratory step), almost all of the 2023–26 clustering comes from two days on which a market drop was followed by a larger one: KOSPI 200 fell 4.1% and then 9.6% on 2 and 5 August 2024, and 8.3% and then 12.7% on 3 and 4 March 2026. Every portfolio breached on 5 August and 95% on 4 March. These two days account for 297 of the 299 breaches that followed a breach on a market shock day, and for 125 of the 145 portfolios flagged as clustered, which have no other back-to-back breaches. Without breaches on those two second days the clustered share would be about 4%, not 48%. In 2010–15 most such chains fall in August 2011. The COVID crash of 2020, which built up over weeks, produced few: 1.9% next-day breaches after a breach on a shock day.
  • Clustering depended on the period, but coverage did not. FHS breaches clustered in 7.7% of the new portfolios, against 7.0% in 2018–22 and 48% in 2023–26. Its breach rate was 1.16% (95% interval 0.89% to 1.44%), the same point value as in 2023–26 by coincidence, and Kupiec rejected 1 of 300 portfolios.
  • The remedy did what it was built for. Raising the next day’s VaR by 1.45 after a breach without a market shock cut the breach rate on those days from 2.9% to 0.55%. Quantile loss was unchanged (−0.004%, 95% interval −0.21% to +0.15%), Kupiec still rejected 1 of 300 portfolios, and the clustered share fell from 7.7% to 5.0%. The factor was fixed on 2018–22 and not tuned on these data.
  • Earlier model results did not carry over. The LightGBM models fitted on 2018–22 were too cautious in 2010–15: breach rates of 0.71% (BASE) and 0.77% (STRESS), with Kupiec rejecting 30% and 21% of portfolios. STRESS clustered more than BASE (13.7% against 6.0%), with no difference in loss (−0.3%, 95% interval −2.1% to +1.3%). Faster volatility (λ=0.85\lambda=0.85) again cut clustering, to 1.0%, at 3.3% higher quantile loss (95% interval +0.8% to +5.4%).
  • At the 97.5% level FHS breached on 2.65% of days (95% interval 2.17% to 3.16%), with no Kupiec rejections and 17% of portfolios clustered.
  • With a shock threshold of −2.5 both conditions would have held, and with −1.5 neither. Both thresholds were set in advance as sensitivity checks that do not change the verdict.

Engineering decision

Incremental revaluation updates only the changed positions instead of revaluing the whole portfolio. Over five repeated benchmark runs it saved 78–79% of the time at 200 assets, but at six assets, the size of a typical retail portfolio, the difference ranged from −46% to +28% with no consistent gain. Full revaluation already takes milliseconds, so we dropped incremental revaluation from the design.

Ongoing

  • The heavy clustering of 2023–26 traces almost entirely to two days on which a crash was followed by a larger one, so it is not a steady flaw of the engine, and the stress model that cut it there did not help on 2010–15. Only 2 of the 33 market shock days in 2023–26 were followed by another crash, so a next-day warning after every crash would mostly be a false alarm. Showing users the two-day losses of past double crashes may serve them better. A prospective evaluation of the frozen FHS, the post-breach uplift and the stress model from December 2026 is the next test.
  • On new data, the uplift fixed on 2018–22 removed the excess breaches on the day after a stock-specific breach at no cost in accuracy. The product could raise the displayed loss figure, or add a notice, on those days.
  • The prototype uses FHS. Student-t has lower quantile loss and FHS fewer Kupiec rejections, and the model confidence set cannot separate them, so the choice stays open until the product fixes which criterion comes first.
  • It is still open whether customers read the displayed loss figures correctly and whether the numbers in the LLM-written explanations are accurate.
